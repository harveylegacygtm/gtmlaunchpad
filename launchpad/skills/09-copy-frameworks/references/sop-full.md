## Where this fits

By now your campaigns are designed. You write copy one campaign at a time - never one email for everyone. It's the last thing you write before you build the list, and what you can write here decides what data you'll need to find.

So if the copy isn't converting, don't rush to rewrite it. Most "my email doesn't work" problems live higher up - in ICP, offer, or campaign. Walk the diagnostic framework (11-launch's four-pillar diagnostic) before you touch a word.

## **Copywriting Definition:**

Copywriting is very misunderstood. Here’s how we define it:

*Copywriting is the art and science of writing words that move the reader to a desired action.*

And that’s why copywriters are the highest-paid writers. 

Because they get people to do stuff.

Your copywriting has one goal: To move the reader to a desired action

Not to a sale, but to the next step in the funnel

In this case, your copy has one job:

Get the right person to respond to your CTA.

Now that we know what we’re doing … let’s get into the how.

## The Dream Email - your north star

Before any rules or templates, ask one question: what's the best email I could send this one person - the one they'd be surprised to get and actually want to say yes to?

A winning email gets a reply for six reasons. Use them as a checklist. The Dream Email hits all six:

- It sounds human - like one person wrote it to another, not a template blasted to thousands.

- It's relevant to their business - not a generic pitch that could go to anyone.

- It sounds like you did your homework - a specific, true thing they can tell took attention.

- It makes them wonder if you can solve a problem better than what they use now.

- There's a real need - you're poking a pain that's bad enough and often enough to act on.

- It pulls an emotion - curiosity, a little FOMO, recognition, relief. Flat emails get deleted.

**Write the dream version first** - with all the personalization and best-case data you wish you had. Then dial it back to the data you can actually find and afford. The dream email tells you exactly what to go enrich for when you build the list. The difference between the dream and what you can find is the cost call you make there. Start high, build down. Don't start with what's easy to find and call it copy.

## The three copy styles

Your two campaign types each get their own copy. You've got three ways to write it. Build and test what fits - they fail in different spots, and you want them covered.

- **Very personal (signal campaigns).** Leans on the trigger and the Signal-to-Offer data from ICP. The opener names the specific event (the funding, the lawsuit, the new role) and the email reads custom. Highest reply rate - and the most expensive to build, since every line depends on enriched data.

- **Plain offer / free-thing (evergreen).** No signal to lean on, so the offer and the free thing carry the email. It has to work cold to anyone in the ICP. Fewer replies per send, but cheap to build and it scales forever.

- **Direct ask (no free thing) - part of evergreen.** No lead magnet, no free Loom, no audit. You just ask for the call. This works when the offer and niche are strong enough to carry it on their own - common in blue oceans where nobody else is even emailing them. It rides on top of either style above: a personal opener into a direct ask, or a plain offer into a direct ask. Test it against the free-thing version and let replies decide.

Three reasons you test both, not just the personal one:

- You won't always have personal data. You need copy that works cold for the leads where there's no signal.

- The more personalization you pack in, the more it costs to build the list and the harder it is to scale.

- Let the market decide, not your gut. Run both, read replies-per-positive, and let the data tell you how much personalization actually pays for itself in this ICP.

Both styles start from the same Dream Email. The personal version keeps more of it; the plain version dials back to what's true for everyone in the segment. The direct-ask version can use either; it just swaps the free-thing CTA for a straight ask for the call. 

## The four questions every email answers

Every cold email answers four questions, in this order:

- **Why are you reaching out to me?** The opener - a trigger, an observation, a reason this is in their inbox today and not generic mass mail.

- **Why should I care?** The pain or the win - what's in it for them, in their words.

- **Why should I believe you?** The proof - a specific result, a client they'd recognize, a number, a credential.

- **What do I do next?** One specific, easy next step.

Miss any of the four and the email breaks. Most cold emails miss #1 (no real reason for reaching out) or #3 (claims with no proof).

## The email-1 skeleton - line by line

Email 1 does ~80% of the work, so it gets a tested structure. Each line earns the next. Your only job is to keep them reading.

- **Subject (1–3 words).** Make it feel like an internal note, not marketing. "billing ops" beats "Scaling Your Brand."

- **Opener - why you.** Anchor to a signal or an insight, but don't force it. "A lot of new VPs I talk to are reworking the stack in their first 90 days - is that on your radar?" beats "Saw you got promoted, congrats!"

- **Problem framing.** A real, specific pain - ideally a small story, not a generalization. "Talked to another ops team in Tampa last week stitching reports together by hand for hours" beats "Most teams struggle to scale data ops."

- **Proof.** Show you can actually solve it. A real result for a real client - "We helped {case_study} {result}." Pick one flavor from the Flavors of Social Proof below, don't default to the same one every time.

- **Value prop / lead magnet.** Plain words, the one thing that's different, framed as what they can now do. Not a feature list.

- **CTA - a gift, not a pitch (soft).** Offer something useful (a 3-min Loom, an audit, a benchmark) or an easy yes/no. "Want me to send the 3-min Loom we made for another ops team?" beats "Quick chat?" In a blue ocean you can sometimes just ask for the call (the direct-ask play above) - test every CTA and see what wins.

- **Sign-off.** A real name. One person to another.

- **P.S.** Hint at the outcome waiting after the magnet, or the wider offer behind it.

- Blue Ocean: A less competitive space, where there are minimal-no other companies selling the same thing you’re selling in the same way to the same people.

## The copy principles that make it human

These are what separate copy that gets read from copy that gets deleted. Use them as a checklist on every email.

- **Sell the superpower, not the sword.** People don't want your features. They want what they can now do because of them. The formula: you do X and Y happens - Y is the superpower. Name the before and after.

- **Solve an expensive problem.** Problems aren't equal. Pick ones that are bad and frequent. A pain they barely notice won't move budget.

- **Write for one person, not a persona.** Copy that feels copy-pasted makes the reader feel like one of thousands, so they don't bother. Show what drew you to them.

- **Steal your prospect's words.** Your customers are better copywriters than you. Pull their exact words from reviews, won deals, and call transcripts. Keep a running "lingo library." The test: would a customer actually say this out loud?

- **Throw rocks at their enemies: use ****"****without.****"** The format: (the result they want) without (the thing that sucks). "Book more meetings without cold calling." The word "without" is what makes it land.

- **Be Specific.** Specific is believable. A vague claim reads as filler. A precise detail puts a picture in their head.

- **Poke the bear.** Show the cost of doing nothing. Losing stings about twice as much as winning feels good, so a sharp question about what they're losing beats a pitch. Let them talk themselves into it.

- **Make it EASY to say yes. Soft CTAs all day..** Don't ask a stranger for 30 minutes Tuesday at 3. Ask an easy yes/no - "worth a look?" - or hand them a gift. Big asks create friction. Friction kills replies.

- **Cut the fluff.** Delete "just," "that," and extra "and/but." Take two passes and cut 10%. If a sentence still works without a word, the word goes.

- **Detach.** Needy copy ("just checking in," "did you get my email?") repels. Write like it's fine if they say no. This is also why you stop at three emails - chasing past that reads as desperate.

- **Paint a picture. **They should be able to visualize the benefit of what you’re offering

- **Good**: You’ll see an extra 1-2 appointments per day on your calendar

- **Bad:** You’ll grow your business

## Tone and format rules - strong defaults

Most winning copy follows these. Treat them as defaults. Break them only when it clearly serves the six reply triggers - not out of laziness.

- **Length: 40–80 words.** 100 is the max. If you can't say it in 80, the offer isn't focused enough - fix the offer, not the copy.

- **Reading level: 5th–6th grade.** They're skimming on a phone between meetings. Run drafts through a readability check; anything grade 7+ gets simpler.

- **Plain text only.** No bold, italics, colors, images, or fancy fonts. Formatting trips spam filters and screams "marketing."

- **One CTA.** Every extra ask cuts your reply rate. Pick one.

- **Sound like a person.** Read it out loud. If it sounds like a press release, rewrite it. If it sounds like texting a smart friend, you're close.

- **Mobile-first.** 60%+ read it on a phone. Short lines. No walls of text.

## Words and phrases to cut

These read as AI or marketing on sight. Strip them:

- **Hype words:** unlock, supercharge, elevate, embark, unleash, revolutionize, transform, game changer, deep dive, skyrocket, seamless, cutting-edge, robust, leverage (as filler).

- **AI-ism phrases:** "Here's the kicker," "let's unpack this," "the key takeaway is," "at the end of the day," "in today's fast-paced world," "it's not just about X - it's about Y," "this changes everything," "but here's the thing," "I came across your profile," "hope this finds you well."

- **Jargon they'd never say out loud:** "synergies," "scalable solutions," "unlock insights," "streamline," "optimize." Use their words instead.

## Subject line rules

The subject is the first filter. Get past it or the email is invisible.

- 1–3 words ideal, 5 max. Short looks like internal email. Long looks like marketing.

- Quiet curiosity, not promo. "quick favor" works; "🚀 GROW 10X 🚀" gets filtered.

- Avoid spam triggers: no ALL CAPS, no exclamation marks, no money symbols, no words like "free," "guaranteed," "limited time."

- Lowercase, internal-note feel often wins: "billing ops," "attribution," "quick question."

## The sequence - three emails, hard cap

Default and ceiling: **three emails**, 2–3 days apart. Three is a hard cap, not a starting point. Emails 1 and 2 stay in one thread. Email 3 you can keep in that thread or start fresh as a new thread - test both.

- **Email 1 - the workhorse.** ~80% of positive replies come from here. Test 3–4 versions in parallel, find the winner, scale it.

- **Email 2 - different angle, same person.** Don't repeat the pitch. New pain, new proof, or a smaller ask (a 1-min Loom instead of a call). ~15% of replies.

- **Email 3 - the breakup.** Short, names the silence, leaves the door open or do a complete 180 and pitch a new angle. ~5% of replies - and it often pulls back people who felt awkward ghosting someone polite. 

Sending it as a new thread with a fresh subject (not "Re:") often works better - it lands like a new message instead of more of the old one, so people who tuned out the first thread actually see it. In-thread, lean on naming the silence. Fresh thread, lead with the new angle.

Don't add a fourth. If three aren't landing, the problem is the offer, ICP, or campaign - not the sequence length. More touches just risk spam complaints and read as desperate.

## The five strategic copy moves

These change what an email says, not just how it sounds. Use them to build email-1 versions and to vary copy across campaigns. Test the angle, not single words - big differences, not word swaps (see "eggs vs flour" in Campaign Architecture).

- **Chunk up vs down.** Broad value prop ("we help Shopify stores grow") vs. ultra-specific ("we recover 10% of wasted ad spend with a bot-traffic filter"). Crowded markets usually want specific; new markets often need the broad context.

- **Mechanism vs no mechanism.** Outcome only vs. explaining the how. Red ocean wants the mechanism (they've heard every outcome claim). Blue ocean is fine on outcome alone.

- **Handle the objection in the copy.** Name the top objection and disarm it: "Most of our best clients had a vendor when we connected - we just showed them the gap."

- **Different pokes.** Each version pokes a different pain. Different people respond to different pokes - spread them and learn which lands.

- **Different proof.** Case-study proof, logo proof, number proof, method proof. Test which one your ICP trusts most.

## AI personalization - done right

Personalization is the bridge between a list and a reply. Without it, even good copy reads as generic mass mail. But the bar has moved. Five years ago, "{first_name}" and "{company_name}" was personalization. Today every cold email does that. Real personalization in 2026 is a specific observation or trigger that proves you actually looked.

**Doesn't work:** generic AI compliments ("I love {company}'s mission"), praise that fits anyone ("impressive growth!"), and things they already know ("I see you're in SaaS"). These tell the reader the rest is automated - worse than no personalization.

**Works:** specific facts they wrote or did ("saw your post on attribution last Tuesday"), recency ("raised Series B last week" beats "raised Series B"), and connections they didn't expect ("your podcast point about X is exactly what we fix").

**The ****"****could this be sent to anyone?****"**** test.** Read your personalization line out loud. If you could send the same line to a different prospect and it still makes sense, it isn't personalization - it's filler. "I love what {company} is doing" fails. "Noticed your post about cold-email burnout last Tuesday" passes.

**Patterns to pull from** (pick 3–5 that fit your ICP and rotate them; which ones you can use depends on the Signal-to-Offer Map and the data you enrich in List Building (SOP 10)):

- **Problems: **If you’re an ad agency, you can detect and call out a problem in their ads. If you’re a business consultant, you can detect a problem in their business. If you’re a SaaS, you can use builtwith to analyze their tech stack and find a gap your SaaS solves. 

- This is often the most effective because it ties into the value proposition. It’s not personalization for personalization's sake. 

- Just be careful with how you address the problem. Nobody wants to be told their baby is ugly.

- **Triggers/observations:** recent funding, new hire or new-in-role, headcount growth, hiring for a relevant role, product launch, leadership change, award, recent podcast appearance.

- **Content:** a recent LinkedIn post, a company blog post, a comment on someone else's post, a conference talk, a quote in a publication.

- **Company signals:** tech-stack change, new G2 reviews, a partnership, a new office, a pricing-page change, specific job postings, a lookalike customer.

- **Industry signals:** a new regulation, competitor news, an analyst report - usually the framing around a sharper individual or company trigger.

- **Personal (use sparingly, never creepy):** mutual connection, shared alma mater, same city, past employer. Public, professional only.

Personalization lives in the opener - it answers "why are you reaching out to me." One sentence. A 20-word personalization line in a 60-word email throws the whole thing off. The AI writes the line at scale; the data behind it comes from enrichment in List Building.

If your offer is strong enough, personalization can be little to none. But most companies we work with are in a red ocean. Personalization is what makes someone give you a shot to explain why you're different.

## The claims and statistics rule

Never invent or fake numbers, client names, results, or case studies. This protects your credibility and keeps you out of trouble.

- Use stats and examples only from your own materials (onboarding, call transcripts, notes, sales calls, testimonials) or reliable, citable industry sources.

- If a number would help but you don't have one, use a believable general claim ("keeping a client costs far less than winning a new one") or drop in a placeholder - {STAT_NEEDED}, {CLIENT_RESULT}, {SOURCE_LINK} - for you to fill in later.

- Stick to your own proof and the case studies you're cleared to use. Don't borrow logos or results that aren't yours.

- USE SPECIFIC NUMBERS. Rounded numbers sound fake.

- Good: $4,023

- Bad: $4,000

## Use the “Flavors of Social Proof” to make them believe your claim

The essence of copywriting is making a claim and then proving it.

Any Joe Schmoe can make a claim, “Grow your business 10x”.  How do you back it up? 

Assume your prospect is a skeptical Karen and has heard your claim 100x before. Write to that person.

Where some go wrong is assuming there’s only 1 or 2 ways to share social proof. There are many, and we call this the Flavors of Social Proof

Here are the different flavors, and examples

- **Case study numerical: **John went from 6 booked calls to 32 within 2 months

- **Case study emotional:** John went from stressed about making payroll, to having his accountant ask him where all this new money is coming from.

- **Aggregate data:** We’ve saved over $40M collectively for our clients by optimizing their tax strategy

- **Average data: **Our average client saves $32,034 per quarter on taxes.

- **One-off data: **We helped John save $40,032 that would have otherwise went to US Government.

- **Name-dropping: **Coca Cola used our strategy and it’s half the reason they’re successful

- **Experience: **Our teams had a combined 40 years experience in media buying.

- **Awards: **We were voted top agency in Utah

- **Reviews: **2301 5-star reviews on Google

## Different copy per campaign

Each campaign is one ICP × one offer × one persona × one playbook, so each one gets its own copy. CRO copy is not Head-of-RevOps copy. Audit-offer copy is not done-for-you copy. The combos add up - three ICPs × two offers is six sets of copy, minimum. That's the point: splitting them up forces you to do the copy work for each segment instead of papering over it with one mediocre "universal" email. Universal emails read as generic to everyone.

## Close the loop - sentiment analysis

Every reply and every sales call is data. Most teams collect both and use neither. The loop:

- **Dump replies into AI.** Every 50–100 replies (yes, no, objection), ask Claude: what patterns, what objections, what words are prospects using?

- **Dump call transcripts into AI.** Every 10–20 calls (via Fathom or similar), ask: what objections come up, what words describe the problem, what proof lands?

- **Update copy with their words.** Replace your guesses with their language. Put the top objections into next month's versions. This is the loop that separates okay copy from great copy.

## Common copy mistakes

- **Too long.** Over 100 words gets skimmed and lost. Cut hard.

- **Claims, no mechanism or proof.** "We help X grow Y." Sounds like every other email.

- **Generic AI personalization.** If it sounds like AI, it hurts more than it helps. Pass the could-be-anyone test.

- **Personalization that doesn't connect to the pitch.** The opener has to lead into the pain or value prop, not just decorate.

- **Too many CTAs.** Pick one. Every extra ask kills your reply rate.

- **Reusing copy across campaigns.** Different copy per persona and offer. No shortcuts.

- **Skipping the sequence - or overrunning it.** Sending email 1 once throws away the other 20% of replies. Going past three reads as desperate. Run exactly three.

- **Inventing proof.** Made-up numbers or borrowed logos. Use real proof or a placeholder.

## Action items

- For your top campaign, write the Dream Email - the best possible email to one person - and check it against the six reply triggers.

- Actually go onto Linkedin, find a real prospect, and write it to them.

- Dial it into two versions: very personal (signal) and plain (evergreen). Note what data each one needs.

- Check every email against the four questions. Fix anything missing.

- Build 3–4 versions of email 1 using different strategic moves (opener, pain, proof, mechanism).

- Write email 2 (different angle) and email 3 (breakup). Stop at three. Decide if email 3 runs in-thread or as a fresh thread, and test it.

- Run drafts through the human-voice checklist and the cut-list. Read aloud. Mark where you'd stop reading.

- Set up the sentiment loop - a monthly AI review of replies and call transcripts.

When that's done, you have a copy system per campaign - and a clear list of the data you need to find.
